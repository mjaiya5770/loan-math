Rent vs Buy: Running the Break-Even Math Honestly

Rent is not "throwing money away" and buying is not automatically smarter. The honest break-even method: compare total costs over the years you will actually stay, including the costs both sides hide.

How-to · Published November 8, 2026 · Loan Math

Estimates only — not financial advice. Rates, taxes and fees vary by lender and state; confirm every figure with your lender before borrowing.

Kill the slogans before you run any numbers

The rent-versus-buy debate is full of one-liners that collapse under a spreadsheet. The two big ones are "rent is money thrown away" and "a mortgage payment is an investment." Both are wrong enough to send you toward a decision you would not make if you saw the full ledger.

Rent buys housing and flexibility. You pay a monthly amount, someone else fixes the roof, and when your job or life changes, you can leave without paying a sales commission or waiting for a buyer. That is a real product, not a waste.

On the other side, a mortgage payment is not a deposit into your own pocket. Early in a 30-year loan, the overwhelming share of each payment covers interest, property tax, and insurance. The principal portion — the part that actually builds equity — starts small and grows slowly. Treating the whole payment as savings overstates the buy side just as badly as treating rent as a total loss understates the rent side.

The honest comparison is about totals over a realistic stay, not slogans.

The honest cost stack for renting

Renting has fewer line items than buying, but that does not make the list trivial. Here is what actually leaves your account.

Monthly rent. This is the headline number and the largest single outflow. It buys shelter, maintenance, and the option to move without selling costs.

Rent increases. A few percent a year is a reasonable planning assumption. Over a five- or ten-year horizon, those increases compound and change the total more than people expect.

Renter's insurance. Small, but real. You are covering your belongings and liability, not the building, so the premium is generally modest.

The deposit and exit. Your upfront deposit is usually one or two months of rent, and leaving costs little. That cheap exit is the flexibility you are paying for with every renewal.

The honest cost stack for buying

The buy side looks simple when you see the monthly payment and stop there. It is not simple. Here are the pieces that belong in the model.

Mortgage interest. This is the loudest line item and usually the largest total cost. On a $360,000 loan at 6.5% over 30 years, total interest alone is $459,160.16. You pay back the $360,000 and then $459,160.16 more, just for the privilege of borrowing it. You can get the true PITI payment for your scenario with our Mortgage Calculator.

Property tax and homeowner's insurance. In our standard example, those run $4,500 and $1,500 per year respectively. They continue for as long as you own the home, and both tend to rise.

Maintenance. Budget real money every year, not just the years something breaks. A roof, a water heater, a furnace — none of these wait for a convenient month.

Buying and selling costs. Closing costs when you buy are real money out the door. When you eventually sell, expect another round of transaction costs. These are ignored in most casual comparisons, and they are the main reason short stays tilt toward renting.

Minus what comes back. The buy side is not all outflow. The principal you pay down each month converts cash into equity, and home-price appreciation can add more. The key is to subtract these honestly: one is slow to build, and the other is not guaranteed.

The invisible line item: opportunity cost

Both camps tend to skip the same thing: the down payment and closing cash could have been invested instead. That is a real cost of buying, even if no invoice arrives.

A fair model charges the buy side for that. Take the same dollars you would put into a down payment and closing costs, assume they earn a reasonable return elsewhere, and the gap between buying and renting shifts. In some years, the forgone return is larger than the principal you build. A rigorous comparison includes that line item; a slogan never does.

If your rent-versus-buy spreadsheet does not include opportunity cost, maintenance, and selling costs, it is not a break-even analysis. It is a cheering section.

The break-even year

Buying carries heavy upfront and exit costs. Renting carries a monthly payment that grows but never stops. The practical consequence is simple: renting usually wins for short stays, buying pulls ahead over longer ones, and the crossover point is the whole ballgame.

The deliverable of this exercise is finding your crossover year for your expected stay.

How to run your own numbers

  1. Pick your realistic stay first. If you are likely to move in three years, model three years. The break-even year means nothing if you ignore it.
  2. Be conservative on appreciation. Plan as if home prices rise slowly, or not at all. Treat strong appreciation as a bonus, not the plan. If buying only works because you assumed aggressive price growth, you are not comparing housing — you are speculating.
  3. Distrust any math that omits maintenance or opportunity cost. Those two line items are where the buy side quietly loses ground in casual spreadsheets and online chatter.

Before you fall in love with a listing, check the income side with our Home Affordability Calculator. A break-even chart does not matter if the payment stretches your budget beyond what a lender or your own nerves will allow.

The point is not to prove that renting or buying is always right. It is to find the year the two lines cross for the stay you are actually planning — and to walk into the decision knowing which side of that year you are on.

Frequently asked questions

How long do I need to stay for buying to win?

It varies with prices, rates, taxes and appreciation in your market — there is no universal number of years. That is exactly what the break-even year in the calculator tells you; short stays usually favour renting because buying's upfront and exit costs have no time to be earned back.

Is the down payment a cost of buying?

Not a consumed cost — it becomes your equity in the home. But the opportunity cost is real: that cash is no longer invested elsewhere, and an honest comparison charges the buy side for it.

Should expected appreciation decide it?

No. Run the numbers with modest appreciation and treat anything stronger as a bonus. A purchase that only works if prices climb fast is a speculation, not a housing decision.
