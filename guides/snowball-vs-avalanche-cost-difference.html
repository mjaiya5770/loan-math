Snowball vs Avalanche: What the Order Actually Costs

Both methods pay the same minimums on everything and attack one debt at a time — the only difference is which one. On a $15,500 stack of three debts, that choice is worth $359.21 and one month. The full race, priced.

Guide · Published November 8, 2026 · Loan Math

Estimates only — not financial advice. Rates, taxes and fees vary by lender and state; confirm every figure with your lender before borrowing.

Every debt-payoff method that works shares the same machinery: list the debts, pay every minimum, and aim all spare cash at one target at a time. Snowball and avalanche differ in exactly one decision — how the target is chosen. The avalanche aims at the highest APR. The snowball aims at the smallest balance. Everything else is identical, which means the entire debate reduces to a measurable question: what does the choice of order cost? This page runs one debt stack through both orders and reads the receipt.

Why order changes cost at all

Interest is rent charged per dollar per month. A dollar owed at 24.99% rents for roughly twenty-five cents a year; a dollar at 7.99% rents for eight. The dollars you retire first should be the ones charging the most rent, because any month a high-rate dollar survives while a low-rate dollar dies early is rent paid for nothing. That is the whole theory of the avalanche. The snowball's theory is not about rent at all — it is about momentum, and we will price that too.

Reading the race

The interest gap is $359.21. That is the exact price of attacking the cheap debt first on this stack: while the snowball spends its early months retiring 7.99% dollars, the 24.99% card keeps renting at full price. The snowball clears the credit card in month 26; the avalanche had it gone in month 23.

The time gap is one month — 39 against 40. Order barely moves the calendar here because the budget, not the sequence, does most of the work.

The first-victory gap is the snowball's real product. The snowball clears its first debt in month 7 — the store card, gone, one fewer bill. The avalanche's first fully cleared debt arrives in month 23. Sixteen months is a long time to wait for a win, and the snowball exists because many households do not last that long. Now that product has a price tag: on this stack, the early win costs $359.21.

One honesty note about the setup: this race was built to diverge. When the smallest balance is also the priciest debt, the two orders pick the same first target, coincide for most of the run, and the debate is moot. The baseline that dwarfs the debate

Pay only the minimums on this same stack and it takes 117 months and $11,503.20 of interest to clear — nearly a decade, and three times the interest of either plan. Set against that baseline, committing a fixed $500 a month is worth roughly $7,700 against the avalanche figure. The order debate is worth $359.21. The fixed-budget decision matters an order of magnitude more than the ordering decision, and it is not close. The verdict, for a cost-focused reader

The avalanche is never the more expensive order: on any set of debts and any budget, its interest is less than or equal to every other sequence. The snowball is a behavioural tool, and on these numbers its cost is $359.21 and one month. Whether that is expensive depends entirely on the alternative actually available to the household — not the ideal one. A household that will finish the snowball and quit the avalanche in month ten comes out ahead of both baselines by thousands. That sentence is arithmetic, not encouragement: the cheapest plan is the one that is still running in month 39.

Frequently asked questions

Is the avalanche always cheaper?

It is never more expensive. On the same debts and the same budget, attacking the highest rate first produces interest less than or equal to any other order — equal when the orders coincide, as they do whenever the smallest balance is also the priciest.

Why is the gap sometimes tiny?

Small balances, close-together rates and short timelines compress it — there is little rent to save when the debts are similar and brief. With wide rate spreads and big balances the gap widens fast, which is why it should be priced on your own stack rather than assumed.

Does paying minimums only ever work?

It clears this stack eventually — in 117 months, at $11,503.20 of interest. That is precisely why the fixed-budget decision dwarfs the ordering decision: minimums-only costs about $7,700 more than the avalanche plan here, against a $359.21 gap between the two orders.
