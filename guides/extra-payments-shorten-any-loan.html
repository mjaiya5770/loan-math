Extra Payments: The Cheapest Way to Shorten Any Loan

Every extra dollar goes straight to principal — and on a $300,000 mortgage, $200 a month extra erases 81 payments and saves $91,173 of interest. The math of prepaying, done honestly.

Guide · Published November 8, 2026 · Loan Math

Estimates only — not financial advice. Rates, taxes and fees vary by lender and state; confirm every figure with your lender before borrowing.

Why extra payments are supercharged

On a long installment loan, the scheduled payment is not split evenly over time. Early on, most of each payment goes to interest because the balance is still large. Every dollar of interest is the cost of carrying the principal for another month.

An extra payment breaks that pattern. If you send money beyond the scheduled amount and mark it as principal-only, it skips interest entirely and reduces the balance directly. From the next month on, the lender calculates interest on a smaller balance. That means each regular payment includes a little less interest and a little more principal than the original schedule assumed.

The mechanism is simple:

  • A lower balance produces less interest.
  • Less interest means more of your next scheduled payment can reduce principal.
  • More principal reduction makes the next month's balance even lower.

It is not a complex renegotiation. It is just moving yourself forward in the amortization schedule without making every scheduled payment.

A $300,000 mortgage at 6% over 30 years

The loan has a scheduled payment of $1,798.65. Over 360 months, total interest is $347,514.57. That is the baseline: no extra payments.

Three shapes of extra payments

Fixed monthly add-on: set-and-forget. You add the same amount each month, such as $100 or $200, and the loan simply ends sooner. This is usually the easiest to automate.

One annual lump: if your money arrives irregularly — like a tax refund or a work bonus — applying it once a year still removes principal and shortens the term. A January lump has more time to reduce interest than a December lump of the same size.

A single early lump: a one-time payment in a specific month, like $5,000 at month 12. It does less than years of monthly extras, but it still saves interest because the balance drops permanently.

Early money beats late money for a plain reason: interest is charged on the balance you owe. A dollar of principal removed today stops generating interest today. The same dollar removed later still helps, but it has fewer months to work. The first-payment anatomy that makes it click

On this loan, the first scheduled payment of $1,798.65 is split into $1,500.00 of interest and $298.65 of principal. That is one of the clearest illustrations of how little principal is repaid early in a 30-year term.

Now add $200 extra to the first payment as principal-only. The month one principal reduction becomes $498.65. That is more than double the scheduled $298.65. You have not doubled your total payment, but you have more than doubled the part that actually reduces the balance.

That same $200 also lowers the balance for every following month. The original amortization table would have charged interest on a larger amount. After the extra payment, each future payment puts slightly more toward principal and slightly less toward interest.

Honest caveats

Extra payments are powerful, but they are not the right move for every situation.

Check for prepayment penalties. These are rare on modern U.S. mortgages, but they can exist on some other types of loans. If an early payoff fee is larger than the interest saved, the math is less attractive.

Keep an emergency fund first. Do not send every spare dollar to a loan if a job loss, medical bill or major car repair could leave you short. An extra principal payment cannot be easily pulled back out later.

Money prepaid into a house is illiquid. It lowers your debt and increases your equity, but it is not a savings account. You cannot spend it without selling, refinancing or borrowing against the property.

Extra principal only works if the servicer applies it to principal. Mark extra amounts "principal only" — on the payment itself, in the loan app, or with a call to the servicer — so the money is not quietly treated as an early regular payment, which would barely move your payoff date at all.

Cards and smaller loans: same mechanism, faster feedback

Nothing about this is mortgage-specific. On a credit card or personal loan the same rule holds: interest is charged on the balance, so every extra dollar aimed at the balance cuts the most expensive thing you own — next month's interest charge. The feedback is simply faster, because the balances are smaller and the payoff date moves in months you can count on your fingers. Our Debt Payoff Calculator runs the same analysis for cards and other debts: balance, APR, your payment and any extra, out comes the debt-free date and the interest avoided.

The order of operations that falls out of the math is boring, which is why it works: keep a starter emergency fund, check the loan has no prepayment penalty, aim every spare dollar at principal — highest-rate debt first if you have several — and let the schedule do the compounding in your favour for once.

Frequently asked questions

Should I mark extra payments "principal only"?

Yes. Unless the servicer knows the extra is for principal, it may be applied as an advance on future scheduled payments — which leaves the balance, and the interest charged on it, almost untouched.

Is it better to invest the money instead?

It genuinely depends on the numbers. Prepaying earns a guaranteed, risk-free return equal to your loan's interest rate; investing may return more or less and carries risk. The loan side of that comparison is the one figure you can know exactly in advance.

Do extra payments lower my required monthly payment?

No. They shorten the term and cut total interest, but the required payment stays the same unless you refinance or your lender offers a recast.
