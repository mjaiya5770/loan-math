How to Calculate Your Car Loan Payment Before You Visit the Dealer

Walk in knowing the number: how to price a car loan yourself — amount financed after down payment and trade-in, the payment formula, and the term-stretch trick that makes a pricier car look cheaper.

How-to · Published November 8, 2026 · Loan Math

Estimates only — not financial advice. Rates, taxes and fees vary by lender and state; confirm every figure with your lender before borrowing.

You can calculate a car payment before you ever set foot in a dealership. The math is not secret, and doing it yourself means you can compare the dealer’s worksheet against your own homework line by line.

Step 1: Know the amount you actually finance

Start with the sticker price or, better, the out-the-door price. Subtract your down payment and any trade-in credit. Taxes, registration, dealer fees, and add-ons push the amount up, so if you have an out-the-door quote, use that as your starting number.

Worked example: a $32,000 car with $4,000 down and a $3,000 trade-in leaves $25,000 financed. That $25,000 is the loan amount, not the sticker price, and it is the number your payment should be built on.

Use the out-the-door figure when you can. A small difference in fees or sales tax can change the loan amount more than a tiny change in APR.

Step 2: Use the amortizing loan formula

A car loan is an amortizing loan. The same formula works for many installment loans:

M = P × r(1 + r)n / ((1 + r)n − 1)

Here, P is the amount financed, r is the monthly interest rate, and n is the number of months. Convert the APR to a monthly rate by dividing by 12.

For a $25,000 loan at 7.5% APR for 60 months, the monthly rate is:

r = 0.075 / 12 = 0.00625

Plug the numbers into the formula and you get a payment of $500.95 per month.

Over the life of the loan, the total interest is $5,056.92. The total of payments is:

$25,000 + $5,056.92 = $30,056.92

The all-in cost of the car includes that principal plus interest, plus the cash you put down. The trade-in already reduced the amount financed, so it also counts as part of the value you gave up to own the car.

Step 3: Beware the term-stretch trick

Dealers often sell the monthly number. A longer term lowers the payment, but it raises the interest cost. Here is the same $25,000 amount financed compared two ways:

Loan setupPaymentTotal interestTotal of payments
60 months at 7.5% APR$500.95$5,056.92$30,056.92
72 months at 8% APR$438.33$6,559.83$31,559.83
Difference−$62.62+$1,502.91+$1,502.91

Stretching the loan to 72 months at 8% drops the payment to $438.33. That sounds easier month to month. But the interest climbs to $6,559.83 — about $1,503 more than the 60-month loan at 7.5%.

The dealer may quote the lower monthly number, but you should compare the total. Buy the total cost, not the payment.

Step 4: Check the four numbers that matter

When you sit down with a dealer worksheet, bring your own calculation and check these four fields:

  • Amount financed — it should match the out-the-door price minus your down payment and trade-in credit.
  • APR — it should match the rate you modeled, not just a quoted monthly price.
  • Term in months — confirm it is the term you actually agreed to, not a longer one slipped into the worksheet.
  • Total of payments — this is the amount you will pay over the full loan. It should line up with your homework.

If any one of those numbers differs from what you calculated, ask why. There may be a legitimate reason, but you want it explained in dollars and cents before you sign.

Step 5: Run the math at several plausible rates

Your credit tier changes the APR a lender offers. A buyer with strong credit may see a lower rate than someone with thin or damaged credit. You do not need to guess perfectly, but you should run the math at two or three plausible rates before you go.

That gives you a range: a best-case payment, a middle-case payment, and a worst-case payment. When the dealer shows you a number, you will know immediately whether that offer is closer to your best case or your worst case.

A small APR change may feel minor on a monthly basis, but over 60 or 72 months it moves the total interest more than most buyers expect.

Frequently asked questions

Is a longer term ever sensible?

A longer term can lower the payment, which may help if you need cash flow. The honest cost is more interest and a longer period of owing more than the car is worth.

Should the trade-in reduce the taxable price?

In many US states, yes — the trade-in credit can reduce the taxable price of the new car. The rules vary by state, so verify your state’s treatment before assuming the tax benefit.

What APR should I expect?

It depends on your credit and the lender. That is exactly why you should model several rates before you visit the dealer, so the quoted APR does not appear out of nowhere.
